## 1. Structuring the Showcase Presentation

When presenting an AI project to stakeholders, managers, or potential employers, they don't just want to see a wall of Python code. They want a narrative that proves you understand both the business problem and the engineering trade-offs.

A professional presentation follows this strict 7-step narrative:

1. **The Problem:** 
   - State the original, manual, or broken process clearly. (e.g., *"We were spending 15 hours a week manually tagging emails."*)
2. **The Specification (Spec):** 
   - Briefly outline the solution design. Show your 1-page spec covering the input, output, and the chosen architectural pattern (Direct Call, RAG, or Agent).
3. **Live Demo:** 
   - Show the application working! A simple command-line interface or a Streamlit web app is perfect. Prove that it takes input and returns the structured output.
4. **Eval Results (The Testing Harness):** 
   - Do not just say "it works." Show the data from your 5-case evaluation harness. Mention how the AI handled the edge cases and adversarial attacks during your red-teaming phase.
5. **The Judgment Framework:** 
   - Explain your safety guardrails. Show exactly where the "Human-in-the-Loop" sits and how human oversight prevents the AI from executing irreversible actions.
6. **Cost and Latency Estimation:** 
   - Share your metrics. (e.g., *"The system averages 2.5 seconds per request and costs approximately $0.35 per 1,000 API calls."*)
7. **Top 3 Learnings:** 
   - Reflect on the build process. What failed during development? Did you have to rewrite your system prompt to fix a constraint? Did you switch models to reduce latency?

## 2. Submitting the GitHub Portfolio

Your GitHub repository is your resume for the modern AI era. A disorganized repository filled with poorly named files (`test1.py`, `working_final.ipynb`) signals a lack of professional discipline.

**Daily-life example:** Handing a hiring manager a disorganized GitHub repo is like an artist handing a gallery owner a trash bag full of crumpled, unlabelled sketches. A curated GitHub repo is a framed, organized gallery exhibition.

**Beginner-friendly way to remember:** Your code isn't finished until it's readable by a stranger.

To finalize your portfolio, your repository must contain the following polished elements:

### The `README.md`
This is the landing page. It should contain a summarized version of your 7-step presentation narrative. Anyone who lands on the repository should instantly understand what the project does and how to run it.

### The Source Code (`/src` or Notebooks)
Your actual Python scripts or Jupyter Notebooks. Ensure they are cleanly commented, use secure `.env` files for API keys, and follow the orchestration patterns covered in Topic 1.

### The Specification (`spec.md`)
Upload your 1-page specification document showing the original blueprint for the project.

### The Eval Harness (`/evals`)
Include the script or notebook you used to run your 5-case testing harness and your red-teaming adversarial attacks. Showing *how* you tested the AI is often more impressive to employers than the AI itself.

### The AI Decision Journal (`journal.md`)
Include your Architectural Decision Records (ADRs). Document the major pivots you made during the project. (e.g., *"We initially tried to build a fully autonomous agent, but realized the latency was too high. We pivoted to a chained-call pattern."*)

### Final Reflection
End your repository with a short reflection on the ethical implications of your project (referencing the frameworks from Week 5 and Week 9), ensuring that you have built an application that is not only functional, but responsible.
