# Case Studies

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Describe three real-world AI failures and explain what went wrong in each one
- Apply the three-question Judgment Framework to each case and identify exactly where the human checkpoint was missing
- Name the three failure patterns — missed override point, tolerated failure mode, diffused accountability — and recognise them in new situations
- Evaluate what a better-designed version of each system would have looked like
- Use these cases as reference points when designing oversight for any AI system you build

---

## Overview

You have learned the theory — how humans think, what biases we carry, how to apply the Judgment Framework, and how to define safe boundaries. This topic is where all of that becomes real.

These are three actual AI failures, from three different industries, that caused real harm to real people. In each case, the AI model itself was not necessarily "broken." The failure was in how humans designed — or failed to design — the oversight around it.

As you read each case, ask yourself: which of the three Judgment Framework questions would have caught this failure, if anyone had asked it?

## Case Study 1 — AI in College Admissions

### What Happened

In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic forced the UK to cancel A-Level exams — the national school-leaving exams that determine university admissions. Since students could not sit the exams, the UK exam regulator (Ofqual) used a statistical algorithm to predict what grades students *would* have achieved.

The algorithm used two main factors:
1. The historical performance of the student's school over previous years
2. The ranking of the student provided by their teachers

### What Went Wrong

Because the algorithm heavily weighted the school's historical performance, high-performing students at historically underperforming schools — often in poorer areas — had their grades systematically downgraded. A student who was clearly exceptional at a school that had performed poorly in the past got a lower predicted grade, regardless of what their teacher said.

Meanwhile, students at smaller, historically high-performing private schools were more likely to keep their teacher-predicted high grades.

**The result:** Nearly 40% of all predicted grades were downgraded by the algorithm. Thousands of students lost their university places.

### Where Was the Human Override Point Missing?

Think of it like this — imagine a teacher has been watching a student work hard all year and gives them a predicted A grade. Then a computer comes along and changes it to a C, based on how other students at the same school did three years ago. And nobody stops to check whether that makes any sense for this individual student.

That is exactly what happened. There was:
- **No individual review** — the system processed thousands of grades simultaneously without any mandatory human check for students whose algorithm grade dropped significantly below their teacher's prediction
- **No immediate appeal** — when students received wrong grades, there was no fast human override process. The default response was "the computer says no"

### Judgment Framework Applied

| Question | Answer in this case |
|---|---|
| **Q1 — Cost of being wrong?** | Very high — a wrongly downgraded grade meant a student lost their university place. Not reversible overnight |
| **Q2 — Can I verify without the AI?** | Yes — a human admissions officer or teacher can read a student's work and judge independently |
| **Q3 — Who is accountable if this fails?** | Nobody was clearly named — so nobody enforced any check |

**The Lesson:** Q1 (high cost) and Q2 (verifiable independently) together tell you this decision needed a mandatory human checkpoint — not an optional one. Q3 tells you that checkpoint must have a named person responsible for enforcing it. None of those three conditions were met.

> **Failure pattern: Missed override point** — the system made high-stakes decisions at scale with no structurally enforced human checkpoint for cases where the algorithm's result conflicted sharply with human judgment.

## Case Study 2 — Automated Medical Triage

### What Happened

A major US healthcare system used an algorithm developed by Optum to identify which patients would benefit most from extra nursing support and care management programs — the kind of support given to patients with the most complex medical needs.

To predict who had the highest healthcare needs, the algorithm used one main proxy: **historical healthcare costs**. The assumption was that patients who had cost more in the past were the sickest and needed the most future care.

### What Went Wrong

The algorithm systematically gave Black patients lower risk scores than white patients with the exact same level of illness.

Why? Because of long-standing historical inequalities and unequal access to healthcare, Black patients typically generated lower healthcare costs than equally sick white patients. The algorithm saw lower costs and concluded: "this person is healthier." It was not reading actual health — it was reading the financial footprint of a biased system.

**The result:** A Black patient had to be significantly sicker than a white patient to receive the same risk score and get access to the extra nursing support they needed.

Think of it like this — imagine you are measuring how hungry someone is by how much food they ate last week. But one person had less food available to them, so they ate less. Measuring past food intake does not tell you who is hungry — it tells you who had access to food. The algorithm made exactly this kind of mistake, with healthcare instead of food.

### What Failure Mode Was Tolerated?

The designers accepted that using "cost" as a proxy for "illness" was good enough. Nobody built in an ongoing audit to check: are the patients we are flagging as low-risk actually doing okay? Or are we systematically missing a specific group?

- **The proxy trap:** When you use an indirect metric (cost) to represent the real thing (illness), you must constantly audit whether the proxy is actually tracking the real thing fairly across all groups. This was not done.
- **The tolerated false negative:** The system was producing false negatives — classifying sick people as healthy — at a much higher rate for Black patients. In medical triage, false negatives are the most dangerous type of error. Accepting them at all, let alone unequally, is a critical failure.

### Judgment Framework Applied

| Question | Answer in this case |
|---|---|
| **Q1 — Cost of being wrong?** | Extremely high — a sick patient classified as low-risk does not get the care they need |
| **Q2 — Can I verify without the AI?** | Yes — a clinician can review a patient's actual health records and symptoms independently |
| **Q3 — Who is accountable if this fails?** | Unclear — no one was auditing whether the proxy metric was producing fair outcomes |

**The Lesson:** Q1 tells you false negatives here are unacceptable. The acceptable error threshold for "missed urgent cases" should have been near-zero — not just "good average accuracy." Q3 tells you someone should have owned an ongoing audit of whether the proxy was working fairly.

> **Failure pattern: Tolerated failure mode** — the system accepted a dangerous type of error (false negatives for a specific group) as an inevitable side effect, rather than treating it as the most critical metric to minimise.

> 📎 [Racial Bias in Health Algorithms — Science (2019)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax2342)

## Case Study 3 — AI Loan Approval at Scale

### What Happened

In 2019, the Apple Card — managed by Goldman Sachs — faced major public backlash. Customers noticed a disturbing pattern: husbands were receiving credit limits up to 20 times higher than their wives, even when they shared the same bank account, filed joint taxes, and the wife had a similar or better credit score.

An automated AI credit assessment system evaluated applicants and assigned credit limits instantly at scale. The algorithm's exact workings were a "black box" — even the customer support staff could not see exactly which factors led to any specific credit limit decision.

### What Went Wrong

When customers called to ask why the limits were so different, the human agents were powerless. They could not explain the decision. They could not override it. Their standard response became: **"It's just the algorithm."**

Think about how frustrating this is from a customer's perspective. You call to ask why your credit limit is 20 times lower than your spouse's. The person on the phone says they cannot tell you why, and they cannot do anything about it. There is no one to talk to. There is no path to a fair resolution.

### Who Was Accountable?

Nobody — and that was the core failure.

- The data science team said: "We built the model correctly."
- The product team said: "We set the policy, not the individual decisions."
- The compliance team said: "The model decides, not us."
- The customer support agents had no authority to override anything.

Everyone pointed to the algorithm. The algorithm cannot be held accountable. So effectively, no one was.

### Judgment Framework Applied

| Question | Answer in this case |
|---|---|
| **Q1 — Cost of being wrong?** | High — a biased credit limit directly affects a real person's financial access and is legally significant at scale |
| **Q2 — Can I verify without the AI?** | In theory yes, but human agents were not given the tools or authority to do so |
| **Q3 — Who is accountable if this fails?** | Nobody. Each team pointed to the algorithm. This is the core failure. |

**The Lesson:** Q3 was never answered. "The algorithm decided" is never an acceptable answer — especially in a regulated industry like finance. Accountability means a specific, named human or role who can explain the decision and has the authority to correct it.

> **Failure pattern: Diffused accountability** — when something went wrong, every human in the system pointed to the algorithm as the decision-maker. Because no one was clearly accountable, no one had the authority or the incentive to fix it.

> 📎 [The Apple Card Algorithm Investigation — Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/the-apple-card-didnt-see-genderand-thats-the-problem/)

## The Three Failure Patterns — Side by Side

| Case | What went wrong | Failure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| UK College Admissions (Ofqual) | High-stakes decisions made at scale with no enforced human checkpoint | **Missed override point** |
| US Healthcare (Optum) | Good average accuracy hid a dangerous failure rate for a specific group | **Tolerated failure mode** |
| Apple Card (Goldman Sachs) | No named human had authority or accountability for the AI's decisions | **Diffused accountability** |

> **The most important insight across all three:** In every case, the AI model itself may have been doing exactly what it was designed to do. The failure was in how humans designed — or failed to design — the oversight around it.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **"The AI is 95% accurate so it is safe to deploy"** — Case Study 2 shows that high average accuracy can hide a completely unacceptable failure rate for a specific group. Always ask: accurate for whom, and wrong in what way?
- **"We have a human in the loop"** — Case Study 3 shows that a human in the loop is useless if that human has no authority to override or explain the AI's decision. Human presence is not the same as human oversight
- **"It's not feasible to review every decision manually"** — the correct answer is not "therefore no oversight." The correct answer is sampled, statistical auditing of the pattern of decisions, with one clearly named person accountable for running that audit

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Human-in-the-loop is not enough on its own** — humans must have the authority to override, the information to understand the decision, and the accountability to act. The Apple Card case shows what happens when humans are present but powerless
- **Proxy metrics are dangerous** — using cost to represent health, or school history to represent individual ability, encodes societal inequalities into AI decisions. Always audit whether your proxy actually tracks the real outcome fairly across all groups
- **High average accuracy can hide dangerous sub-group failures** — always check performance separately for the groups most at risk of harm, not just the overall score
- **Accountability must be named before deployment** — "the algorithm decided" is never an acceptable answer. One specific human role must own each AI decision category and have the authority to correct it
- **A checkpoint that exists only on paper is not a real checkpoint** — checkpoints must be system-enforced, logged, and auditable. If it depends on individual discipline under time pressure, it will eventually stop being exercised

> **Interview tip:** When discussing AI safety in interviews, do not just talk about technical metrics. Use these three failure patterns — missed override point, tolerated failure mode, diffused accountability — to show you understand how AI interacts with human systems. Name the pattern, name the case, explain the fix. This demonstrates systematic judgment, not just opinion.
