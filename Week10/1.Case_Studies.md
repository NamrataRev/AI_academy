# Case Studies

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Identify critical points in AI workflows where human oversight is necessary to prevent cascading errors
- Analyze real-world failures in automated medical triage and understand why certain failure modes must not be tolerated
- Evaluate accountability structures in AI-driven loan approval processes operating at scale
- Apply the lessons from these case studies to design safer boundaries for AI systems in production

---

## Overview

When AI systems are deployed in high-stakes environments—like education, healthcare, and finance—the consequences of failure extend far beyond incorrect data. A missed human override point, a tolerated failure mode, or unclear accountability can cause significant, real-world harm. 

In this topic, we will explore three high-profile case studies that highlight exactly what happens when AI systems operate without adequate safeguards. These examples will ground the theoretical concepts of judgment frameworks and safe boundaries into practical, cautionary lessons. The goal is not just to see where things went wrong, but to understand **why** the systems failed to stop the errors before they impacted users.

---

## Case Study 1: AI in College Admissions — Where Was the Human Override Point Missed?

**The Scenario:**
In 2020, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, exams for A-Level students in the UK were cancelled. To determine university admissions, the exam regulator (Ofqual) used a statistical algorithm to predict what grades students *would* have achieved. 

**How It Worked:**
The algorithm calculated grades based on two main factors:
1. The historical performance of the student's school over previous years
2. The ranking of the student provided by their teachers

**What Went Wrong:**
Because the algorithm heavily weighted the historical performance of the school, high-performing students at historically underperforming schools (often in poorer areas) had their grades systematically downgraded. Conversely, students at smaller, historically high-performing private schools were more likely to keep their teacher-predicted high grades. Nearly 40% of all predicted grades were downgraded by the algorithm.

**Where Was the Human Override Point Missed?**
The failure was not just statistical; it was a failure of oversight.
- **No Individual Review:** The system processed thousands of grades simultaneously without a mandatory human review step for students whose algorithm grade dropped significantly below their teacher's prediction.
- **No Immediate Appeal:** When students received their results, the "computer says no" response was the default. There was no rapid human override point or clear appeals process available, leaving students temporarily locked out of their university offers.
- **The Lesson:** In any high-stakes system affecting human futures, a **human-in-the-loop (HITL)** is mandatory. A system must flag edge cases—like a massive discrepancy between a teacher's human judgment and the algorithm's output—for manual review *before* the final decision is issued.

## Case Study 2: Automated Medical Triage — What Failure Mode Was Tolerated?

**The Scenario:**
A major US healthcare system used an algorithm, developed by Optum, to identify which patients would benefit most from "high-risk care management" programs. These programs provide extra nursing support and resources to patients with complex medical needs.

**How It Worked:**
To predict who had the highest healthcare needs, the algorithm used a proxy metric: **historical healthcare costs**. It assumed that patients who cost more in the past were the ones who were the sickest and needed the most future care.

**What Went Wrong:**
The algorithm systematically discriminated against Black patients. Because of historical inequalities, unequal access to care, and systemic biases, Black patients typically generated lower healthcare costs than white patients with the exact same level of health issues. 
By using cost as a proxy for sickness, the algorithm falsely concluded that Black patients were healthier. A Black patient had to be significantly sicker than a white patient to receive the same risk score and get the extra nursing support.

**What Failure Mode Was Tolerated?**
- **False Negatives in Triage:** The system tolerated a massive rate of false negatives (sick people classified as healthy) specifically for a marginalized group. 
- **The Proxy Trap:** The designers tolerated the assumption that "cost equals illness." In medical triage, accepting a proxy metric without constantly auditing it for bias is a critical failure mode. 
- **The Lesson:** When designing AI for triage, you cannot tolerate false negatives that disproportionately affect specific groups. You must audit the actual outcomes (are sick people getting care?) rather than just the proxy metrics (are we predicting costs accurately?).

## Case Study 3: AI Loan Approval at Scale — Who Was Accountable?

**The Scenario:**
In 2019, the Apple Card (managed by Goldman Sachs) faced massive public backlash. Customers—including high-profile tech founders—noticed a disturbing pattern: husbands were receiving credit limits up to 20 times higher than their wives, even when they shared bank accounts, filed joint taxes, and had similar or better credit scores.

**How It Worked:**
An automated AI credit assessment system evaluated applicants and assigned credit limits instantly at scale. The algorithm's exact inner workings were a "black box," meaning even the customer support agents didn't know exactly which variables led to a specific credit limit.

**What Went Wrong:**
When customers called customer service to ask why the credit limits were so disparate, the human agents were powerless. They could not explain the algorithm's decision, and more importantly, they could not override it. They frequently told customers, "It's just the algorithm."

**Who Was Accountable?**
- **The Accountability Void:** The system was designed for scale and speed, but it completely lacked an accountability structure. When an AI makes a controversial decision, someone must be able to explain *why*. 
- **Disempowered Human Agents:** Human agents were in the loop, but they were entirely disempowered. A human override point is useless if the human is not allowed to override the AI or cannot see why the AI made its decision.
- **The Lesson:** Accountability in AI means explainability and authority. If a system operates at scale, there must be a mechanism for a human to audit the decision and the authority to correct it. "The algorithm decided" is never an acceptable answer in a regulated industry like finance.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Human-in-the-Loop is not enough; it requires authority.** Having humans involved is pointless if they do not have the power to override the AI's decisions, as seen in the Apple Card incident.
- **Proxy metrics are dangerous in high-stakes environments.** Using historical data like "cost" to represent "health" can hardcode societal biases into an AI system, leading to dangerous false negatives in medical triage.
- **Mass automation requires mass appeals processes.** If an algorithm (like the Ofqual grading system) makes decisions for thousands of people instantly, a robust, accessible human appeals process must be built into the workflow from day one.
- **Explainability is accountability.** If your team cannot explain why an AI made a specific decision, you cannot deploy it in a high-stakes scenario. 

> **Interview tip:** When discussing AI safety in interviews, do not just talk about technical metrics. Use these case studies to demonstrate that you understand how AI interacts with human systems. Highlight that true AI safety is about designing proper **workflows, override authorities, and accountability structures**.
