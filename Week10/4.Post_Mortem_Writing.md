# Post-Mortem Writing

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain the purpose of a "blameless" post-mortem in engineering and why it is critical for AI systems
- Structure a formal post-mortem document covering what failed, why it failed, and who was accountable
- Differentiate between a technical root cause (e.g., model hallucination) and a systemic root cause (e.g., lack of human oversight)
- Propose specific governance and design changes to prevent a failure from recurring

---

## Overview

No matter how well you design your AI system, how rigorous your evaluation metrics are, or how carefully you place human-in-the-loop checkpoints—**your AI will eventually fail.** 

When a failure impacts users, money, or reputation, an engineering team must conduct an incident review, commonly called a **post-mortem**. 

A post-mortem is a formal document written after an incident is resolved. Its goal is not to punish the person who deployed the bad code. Its goal is to uncover the systemic weaknesses that allowed the bad code to reach production in the first place. In this topic, we will walk through the structure of an AI post-mortem and how to write one effectively.

---

## The "Blameless" Culture

Before writing a single word, you must understand the golden rule of incident reviews: **Post-mortems must be blameless.**

If an engineer accidentally deploys an untested AI model that deletes customer data, your first instinct might be to blame the engineer. But a blameless post-mortem asks a different question: *Why did our system allow a single engineer to deploy an untested model directly to production without a mandatory review?*

When you blame individuals, people hide their mistakes. When you blame systems, you can fix them. The focus is always on improving the governance, the testing pipelines, and the safety boundaries.

---

## Core Components of an AI Post-Mortem

A standard AI post-mortem contains four main sections.

### 1. What Failed (The Incident Summary)
This is a plain-English explanation of what happened, completely free of jargon. It must include:
- **The Impact:** How many users were affected? Was data lost? Was money lost?
- **The Timeline:** When did the failure start? When was it detected? When was it resolved?
- **The Resolution:** What immediate action was taken to stop the bleeding? (e.g., "We rolled back to the previous model version" or "We turned off the AI and reverted to human support agents.")

### 2. Why It Failed (Root Cause Analysis)
This is the most critical section. In AI, there are usually two root causes you must identify:
- **The Technical Root Cause:** Why did the model do the wrong thing? (e.g., "The training data lacked examples of non-US addresses, causing the model to incorrectly format international shipping labels.")
- **The Systemic Root Cause:** Why did the team fail to catch it before deployment? (e.g., "Our evaluation dataset did not include a diverse geographic test suite, and there was no human-in-the-loop checkpoint for edge cases.")

> **Note:** "The model hallucinated" is **never** an acceptable root cause on its own. Models hallucinate by default. The root cause is *why your system allowed a hallucination to reach the user.*

### 3. Accountability
Blameless does not mean unaccountable. Accountability means clarifying who owned the response and who owns the follow-up tasks.
- Who made the final call to halt the system?
- Who was responsible for communicating with impacted users?
- Who is the designated owner for ensuring the preventative measures are built?

### 4. Governance & Prevention
This section defines what changes will be made to the system's boundaries, evaluations, or human oversight to ensure this exact failure never happens again.
- Do we need a new metric in our evaluation pipeline?
- Do we need to move from "Autonomous" to "Mandatory Sign-Off" for this specific AI feature?
- Do we need to update our Acceptable Use Policy or safety guardrails?

## Worked Example: The Pricing Chatbot Incident

Let's look at a simplified, real-world scenario.

**Scenario:** An e-commerce company deployed an LLM-powered chatbot to answer customer questions. Over a weekend, the chatbot began offering users a 90% discount on premium laptops because it "hallucinated" a promotional policy. Several users completed checkout with the massive discount before the issue was caught.

**Post-Mortem Extract:**

- **What Failed:** Between Saturday 9:00 AM and Sunday 11:00 AM, the AI chatbot incorrectly offered 90% discounts to 45 customers. 12 customers successfully checked out, resulting in a loss of $18,000. We resolved the incident at 11:15 AM on Sunday by hard-disabling the chatbot and reverting to a static FAQ page.
- **Why It Failed:** 
  - *Technical:* The LLM hallucinated a non-existent promotion when users asked aggressive, hypothetical questions about price matching.
  - *Systemic:* Our pre-launch testing only included "happy path" questions (friendly customers). We did not conduct red-teaming (adversarial testing). Furthermore, the chatbot was directly connected to the checkout API, allowing it to apply discounts autonomously without checking our central pricing database.
- **Accountability:** The Lead AI Engineer authorized the rollback. The Customer Success Lead is responsible for contacting the 12 buyers. The Product Manager owns the follow-up roadmap.
- **Governance & Prevention (Action Items):**
  1. *Immediate:* The chatbot's access to the discount API is revoked.
  2. *Design Change:* We are implementing a mandatory API check: the chatbot can only offer discounts that explicitly exist in the `active_promos` database.
  3. *Process Change:* All future AI features must undergo adversarial red-teaming before deployment.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Focus on the system, not the person:** Blaming humans hides errors. Fixing systems prevents them.
- **"Hallucination" is not a root cause:** The true root cause is always a gap in your evaluation pipeline, your guardrails, or your human oversight.
- **Accountability is about ownership:** It means knowing exactly who has the authority to pull the plug and who owns the fix.
- **Post-mortems drive governance:** Every post-mortem should end with concrete, actionable changes to your system's design or deployment processes.

> **Interview tip:** In a behavioral interview (e.g., "Tell me about a time something went wrong"), structure your answer like a post-mortem. Briefly state the impact, explain the root cause clearly, and spend the majority of your time discussing the systemic, preventative changes you implemented afterward.
