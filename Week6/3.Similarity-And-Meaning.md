## Overview

You now know that a vector is a list of numbers describing multiple properties of one thing, and that vectors close together in space represent similar things. This topic gives you the actual mathematical tools to measure "how close" two vectors are — the **dot product** and **cosine similarity** — and introduces **embeddings**, the special kind of vector AI models use to represent the *meaning* of words, sentences, images, and more.

Think about how Spotify knows what song to recommend next. It doesn't just match songs by genre — it compares the mathematical "fingerprint" of songs you've liked against every other song in its library, and finds the ones with the closest fingerprint. That fingerprint is an embedding. The comparison is cosine similarity. This topic teaches you both — by hand, with small numbers — so you understand exactly what is happening inside a real AI system when it searches, recommends, or retrieves information.

## 6.1 The Dot Product — Vectors Pointing the Same Direction Score High

The **dot product** of two vectors is a single number calculated by multiplying each pair of matching components together, then adding up all those products.

**Formula:**

For two vectors `A = [a1, a2, a3]` and `B = [b1, b2, b3]`:

```
A · B  =  (a1 × b1)  +  (a2 × b2)  +  (a3 × b3)
```

- `A · B` — read as "A dot B." The dot (·) is the standard notation for this operation.
- The operation: multiply the 1st component of A by the 1st of B, multiply the 2nd by the 2nd, multiply the 3rd by the 3rd, then **add all three results together.**
- The final answer is always a **single number** — not a vector.

### Worked Calculation — Music Taste Vectors

Two music taste vectors (pop score, rock score), each rated 0–10:

```
A (Ananya) = [4, 3]
B (Kabir)  = [5, 2]
```

```
A · B = (4 × 5) + (3 × 2)
      = 20 + 6
      = 26
```

**How to interpret this:** "26" on its own doesn't tell us much. The dot product's raw value is affected by how large the numbers in the vectors are — not just their direction. A person who rated everything out of 100 instead of out of 10 would get a much bigger dot product, even with an identical taste pattern. This is exactly why the dot product alone is an incomplete similarity measure — which leads us to cosine similarity next.

The core intuition holds though: **when two vectors point in roughly the same direction — their large values line up — the dot product tends to be a bigger positive number.**

## 6.2 Embeddings — Turning Words Into Vectors That Capture Meaning

An **embedding** is a vector (usually with tens or hundreds of components) that represents the *meaning* of a piece of data — most commonly a word, sentence, image, or document — such that things with *similar meaning* end up as vectors that are *close together* in space.

**Why this exists:** Computers cannot compare the meaning of two words like "mango" and "banana" directly — they can only compare numbers. An embedding solves this by placing every word at a specific point in a numeric space, learned by an AI model from studying huge amounts of real text.

During training, the model notices that "mango" and "banana" tend to appear in similar contexts — "I ate a ___ for breakfast," "___ smoothie" — far more often than "mango" and "car." It gradually positions "mango" and "banana" close together in this numeric space, while "car" ends up far away.

**A simplified toy embedding — for intuition only:**

Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions. Let's imagine a simplified 2-dimensional space with dimensions **(is_fruit, is_vehicle)**, each scored 0–10:

```
mango  = [9, 0]
banana = [9, 1]
car    = [0, 9]
```

Even in this toy example, "mango" and "banana" sit close together (both near `[9, 0]`), while "car" sits far away — exactly the pattern that real embeddings produce, just with far more nuanced, machine-learned dimensions.

## 6.3 Why "King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen" Works

This famous example shows that embeddings don't just place similar words near each other — they also capture *relationships* between words as directions in space. This means you can do arithmetic on meaning itself.

**Simplified toy embedding (2 dimensions: royalty_score, gender_score):**

```
king  = [8,  5]
man   = [1,  5]
woman = [1, -5]
```

**Step-by-step vector arithmetic:**

Step 1 — Compute `king − man` (subtract matching components):
```
king − man = [8 − 1,  5 − 5] = [7, 0]
```
This result, `[7, 0]`, represents "pure royalty with the gender component cancelled out" — because both king and man had the same gender score (5), subtracting removed that shared part.

Step 2 — Add `woman` to that result:
```
[7, 0] + woman = [7 + 1,  0 + (−5)] = [8, −5]
```

**Result:** `king − man + woman = [8, −5]`

Now compare this to `queen` in our toy space:
```
queen = [8, −5]
```

They match exactly. In a real embedding space with hundreds of dimensions learned from billions of words of text, the result doesn't land *exactly* on "queen" — but it lands *very close* to it, closer than almost any other word. This is why it is written as an **approximation**: `king − man + woman ≈ queen`.

**Why this works:** `king − man` isolates the "royalty" direction by cancelling the shared "male" component. Adding `woman` then applies that royalty direction onto the "female" starting point — arriving at "royal + female" = queen.

## 6.4 Cosine Similarity — Measuring the Angle Between Two Meaning-Vectors

**Cosine similarity** measures how similar two vectors are by looking at the **angle** between them, ignoring their length — giving a score between −1 and 1:

- **1** = pointing in exactly the same direction — maximally similar
- **0** = at a right angle — unrelated
- **−1** = pointing in exactly opposite directions

**Formula:**

```
cosine_similarity(A, B) = (A · B) / (|A| × |B|)
```

- `A · B` — the dot product of A and B (calculated above)
- `|A|` — the **magnitude** (length) of vector A: for `A = [a1, a2]`, this is `√(a1² + a2²)`
- `|B|` — the magnitude of vector B, calculated the same way
- Dividing by both magnitudes **cancels out the effect of vector length**, leaving a pure measure of direction — this is exactly what fixes the limitation of the raw dot product

### Worked Calculation — Full Example

```
A = [3, 4]
B = [6, 8]
```

Step 1 — Dot product:
```
A · B = (3 × 6) + (4 × 8) = 18 + 32 = 50
```

Step 2 — Magnitude of A:
```
|A| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5
```

Step 3 — Magnitude of B:
```
|B| = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10
```

Step 4 — Cosine similarity:
```
cosine_similarity(A, B) = 50 / (5 × 10) = 50 / 50 = 1
```

**Interpretation:** A cosine similarity of **1** means A and B point in exactly the same direction. Even though B is longer (B = 2 × A), their *direction* — and therefore what they represent — is identical.

### Second Example — Unrelated Vectors

```
A = [3, 4]
C = [4, -3]
```

```
A · C = (3 × 4) + (4 × −3) = 12 − 12 = 0
cosine_similarity(A, C) = 0 / (5 × 5) = 0
```

**Interpretation:** A cosine similarity of **0** means the two vectors are completely unrelated in direction — in a real embedding space, this means the two words or sentences have no meaningful semantic relationship.

### Dot Product vs Cosine Similarity — Side by Side

| Aspect | Dot Product | Cosine Similarity |
|---|---|---|
| Affected by vector length? | Yes | No — cancelled out by dividing |
| Range of possible values | Any number (unbounded) | Always between −1 and 1 |
| What it measures | Direction and magnitude combined | Pure direction — angle only |
| Typical AI use | A building block in other calculations | The standard metric for comparing embeddings |

## Worked Example — RAG System Finding Relevant Products

**Scenario:** An e-commerce RAG assistant needs to find which of two stored product-description embeddings is more relevant to a customer's search query.

```
Query           Q = [2, 2]
Product A desc  A = [4, 4]
Product B desc  B = [1, -1]
```

**Cosine similarity(Q, A):**
```
Q · A = (2×4) + (2×4) = 16
|Q| = √(4 + 4) = √8 ≈ 2.83
|A| = √(16 + 16) = √32 ≈ 5.66
cosine_similarity(Q, A) = 16 / (2.83 × 5.66) ≈ 1.0
```

**Cosine similarity(Q, B):**
```
Q · B = (2×1) + (2×−1) = 0
cosine_similarity(Q, B) = 0
```

**Conclusion:** Product A (similarity ≈ 1.0) is far more relevant than Product B (similarity = 0). This is exactly the calculation a real RAG system performs — just across hundreds of dimensions and thousands of candidate documents instead of two.

## Key Takeaways

- The **dot product** multiplies matching components of two vectors and adds the results — a bigger positive value suggests similar direction, but the raw value is also affected by vector length
- An **embedding** is a vector that represents meaning — semantically similar things end up close together in numeric space
- Vector arithmetic on embeddings (`king − man + woman ≈ queen`) works because embeddings capture relationships as consistent directions in space, not just isolated points
- **Cosine similarity** = dot product ÷ (magnitude of A × magnitude of B) — it measures pure direction, ranges from −1 to 1, and is the standard metric for comparing embeddings
- Cosine similarity of 1 = same direction (maximally similar). 0 = unrelated. −1 = opposite directions
- This exact maths — embeddings + cosine similarity — powers search, recommendations, and RAG

> **Interview tip:** Be ready to compute a dot product and a cosine similarity by hand for two small 2D vectors. This is a very common conceptual check interview question for AI and data roles. Practice the four-step process: dot product → magnitude of A → magnitude of B → divide.
